Dad math starts as a flex. Someone quotes $180 to hang a door, fix a leak, assemble the thing, or "just replace that part." You hear yourself say it before you have even looked in the garage:

"I can do that cheaper."

Sometimes you can. Sometimes you are buying a new personality in the tool aisle. Same wallet as [the dad tax](/guides/the-dad-tax). Different trap: pride with a SKU.

## What dad math actually costs

Write three numbers before you buy anything:

1. **The hire:** what they asked.
2. **The materials:** the part, the wood, the cartridge of whatever.
3. **The tool tax:** anything you do not already own that the job requires. Include the "I'll use it again" items. Be honest.

If 2 + 3 is close to 1, you are not saving. You are shopping. Add a fourth line if you are a grown-up: **the Saturday.** Time is money you already spent at work.

A $12 part plus a $79 tool you will use once is not a win. It is a $91 story you tell at pickup.

## The reuse test

Ask one question: **Will I use this tool again in the next 12 months on a job I already know is coming?**

- Yes, and you can name the job: buy it, or borrow it.
- "Maybe, around the house": that is a no dressed up as a yes.
- You want the tool more than you want the repair: that is a hobby. Hobbies are allowed. Do not call them savings.

Borrowing is dad math that actually works. Neighbor, in-laws, library of things, the guy at work who already owns a wet vac. Returning it on time is part of the deal.

## The two-trip rule

If the project needs a second store run, the savings are leaking. One missing bit is how a two-hour job becomes an afternoon and a $40 impulse bin.

Rule: if you cannot finish with what is in the house plus one planned purchase, pause. Either make the full list (and the full number) or pay the person who already owns the list.

This is the same triage energy as [car vs daycare](/guides/car-vs-daycare). Minimum viable fix. Not a fantasy workshop.

## When the DIY bill passes the quote

Halfway in, you are at $140 on a $180 job with the wall open. Sunk cost will tell you to keep going because you have already spent it. Sunk cost is not a number. It is a feeling with a wallet.

Set the stop rule before you start: **if the running total hits 80 percent of the quote and the job is not close to done, you stop and call.** Write the number on the box of the part so you cannot renegotiate with yourself at hour four.

Stopping well matters more than stopping early:

- **Make it safe overnight.** Water off at the valve, breaker off and labeled, no bare wire, no open hole a kid can reach. Safe beats finished.
- **Do not undo your work.** Leave the demo done, the old part out, the access open. That is real labor a pro will not have to bill you for.
- **Photograph everything before you close anything up.** What you found behind the wall is worth money to the next person holding the wrench.
- **Say the actual words when you call.** No performance:

> "I got partway and hit a wall. Here's what's done, here's what's left."

- **Ask what you can still do.** Most trades will knock something off if you handle demo, haul-off, or the paint after.

> Truth: The bill for quitting at hour three is always smaller than the bill for finishing wrong at hour nine.

## When pride is doing the talking

Pride is not the enemy. Pride is why you still try. It becomes expensive when:

- You have never done this job, and YouTube is your only teacher.
- A mistake can flood, shock, or fail an inspection.
- You are doing it to prove something to a quote, a father-in-law, or a version of yourself from 2014.
- The kid is watching and you would rather look capable than be done by dinner.

You can be a capable dad and still write a check. Those are not opposites. [Thrift without looking like a dare](/guides/thrift-without-looking-like-a-dare) is the same idea in a closet: cheap is a strategy, not a costume, and not every job needs your name on it.

## A simple call

Use this on the hood of the car, out loud if you have to:

- **Pay them** if safety, code, or a second trip is in play.
- **DIY** if you own the tool (or can borrow it), you have done a cousin of this job, and 2 + 3 is clearly under 1.
- **Split it** if you can do the grunt work (demo, painting, hauling) and hire the part that scares you.

"I'll do the prep, you do the valve" is still dad math. It is just dad math that finishes.

## Scripts

> To yourself: "Am I saving money, or am I buying a tool?"

> To the quote: "I can do the easy half. What does that do to the number?"

> To your kid, if they ask why you hired it: "Some jobs need a person who already has the gear. We still did the part we could."

## When this breaks

**Nobody will come out for a job this small.** Plenty of trades carry a minimum service call of $100-$150, and some will not schedule a twenty-minute repair at all. Now the hire number is fake, and DIY is the only real option. Change the math: your ceiling is no longer the quote, it is what a mistake costs. If the worst case is a wasted part, go. If the worst case is water in a wall, keep calling. Try the one-man operators before the companies with a dispatcher, ask the hardware store desk who they send people to, and bundle the small job with another so it clears the minimum.

**The quote is fair and you still cannot pay it this month.** This is not a math problem, it is a cash flow problem, and dad math will not solve it. Sort by consequence instead. If it is getting worse every day (a leak, a roof, brakes), do the cheapest containment now: a bucket and a shutoff valve, a tarp, a bus pass. Then put the real repair on the calendar for the month you can cover it. Many trades will take half now and half in thirty days if you ask directly and early. Asking for terms is not begging. It is how every business in town operates.

## The point

Broke does not mean broken, and it does not mean you have to become a contractor by Friday. The cheap version is the one that is done, safe, and not hiding a $90 saw in the garage for a leak you will not see again. Pride can come to dinner. It does not get the debit card without a vote.
