The first real injury is not a scraped knee with a cartoon bandage. It is the sound they make, the way they look at you, and the quiet car ride where everyone is doing math about what happens next.

You do not need a speech. You need a job.

> Remember: Your kid borrows your temperature. Panic is contagious. So is calm.

## First: the kid

In the moment, three moves beat ten tips.

1. **Get low.** Eye level if you can. Standing over them turns you into a ceiling light and a lecture.
2. **Name the plan, not the fear.** Doctor, ice, car, X-ray. One next step.
3. **Touch without hovering.** Hand on a shoulder. Hold the good hand. Do not poke the bad one "to check."

> To your kid: "I've got you. We are going to take care of this."

> Or: "You are safe. We are going to the doctor now."

Skip "You're fine" if they are clearly not. Skip "This is nothing" if their face says otherwise. Skip the joke that needs them to laugh so *you* feel better.

If they ask "Will it hurt?" tell the truth in one line.

> "It might for a minute. I will be right here."

Same energy as [the after-school collapse](/guides/the-after-school-collapse-is-not-a-bad-kid): snack and steadiness first, interrogation never.

## Second: your partner

Injuries pull couples into old roles fast. One person becomes logistics. One person becomes feelings. Both can resent it by dinner.

**In public (ER, sideline, waiting room):** one team. No blame about the trampoline, the coach, or who was watching. That is a hotel-room conversation, not a lobby show. Same rule as [Disney: us against the kids](/guides/disney-world-us-against-the-kids). The injury is the shared problem. Not each other.

> To your partner: "I got logistics. You stay with them. Tell me what you need."

> Or: "Your turn to breathe. I've got the forms."

**Concrete support that actually lands:**

- Water, charger, protein bar, the other kid's pickup
- Writing down what the nurse said so nobody has to remember under fluorescent lights
- Texting the grandparent once, not a group chat novel
- Asking "Do you want company or space?" and believing the answer

> Remember: "Let me know if you need anything" is not a plan. A ride, a meal, or the night shift is a plan.

If she is the one holding it together and you are spinning, do not make her regulate you too. Step outside. Call a friend. Come back useful.

## Third: the house after

The cast, the crutches, the "no PE for two weeks" note change the week. Protect sleep and routines more than you protect the highlight reel of being the dad who "handled it."

- **One calm adult at bedtime.** Pain, weird sleep positions, and fear stack. Keep the script short, like you would after a hard night in [when you yelled](/guides/when-you-yelled-and-youre-the-last-one-awake).
- **Let them be slower.** Waiting for them to hop to the car is not a character test.
- **Watch the sibling.** The uninjured kid will test whether injury is a cheat code. Fair rules still exist. So does extra patience.

> To your kid: "Rest is part of getting strong. You are not falling behind."

> To the sibling: "They need help. You still have your jobs."

## What not to do

- Do not film the crying for the group chat.
- Do not diagnose from a Facebook aunt.
- Do not compete with your partner about who is more worried.
- Do not turn "walk it off" into a personality. Some kids need ice and an adult who believes them.

If it is a sports field, you can still be the dad from [tagging along without becoming furniture](/guides/tagging-along-without-becoming-furniture): present, useful, not a second coach yelling over the trainer.

## Scripts that fit in your pocket

> To your kid: "Brave is telling us when it hurts."

> To your partner: "Same team. We finish this, then we talk."

> To yourself: "Jobs, not speeches."

## The point

Broke does not mean broken, and a broken bone does not mean you failed as a parent. The cheap version of support is the one that shows up: calm voice, filled forms, cold pack, and a partner who does not have to carry the whole night alone. Your kid will forget half the medical words. They will remember who stayed soft when it hurt.
