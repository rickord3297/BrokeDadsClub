The assignment said "family tree." What arrived was a logistics project: print old photos, buy a trifold board, find glue that actually sticks, and lose a Tuesday you did not have.

By 9 p.m. you are holding the scissors. Your kid is holding a sticker. The poster looks... good. Suspiciously good.

That is the trap. September homework is often parent homework wearing a kid's name.

> Remember: A polished poster teaches them you do not trust their hands.

## Name your actual jobs

**Yours:** calendar, cheap supplies, printing if they cannot, driving to the library, emotional regulation when they melt down.

**Theirs:** pick the layout, write the labels, cut (age-appropriate), paste, present one fact they can explain out loud.

If you would not let them use the table saw alone, stay for scissors. If they can write their name, they can write the caption.

> Truth: Support is teaching the steps. Help is doing the steps while they watch.

## The 20-minute rule

Set a timer for their work block. You sit nearby, not at the board.

When they ask "does this look good," try:

> "Tell me one thing you learned while you did it."

Redirect beats takeover.

When the timer ends, stop. Incomplete and honest beats complete and yours. Email the teacher if needed: "We ran out of steam; here's what they finished."

## Budget the board without [dad math](/guides/dad-math) regret

Trifold board, markers you own, photos from your phone. Total under $12 if you shop once.

Do not buy a kit that builds the project for them. Do not upgrade to foam core because Pinterest existed.

If supplies blow the week, that is [the second bill](/guides/the-second-bill) in craft form. Cap it before you enter the store.

## When other posters look professional

They might be parent-made. They might be kid-made with time you do not have. Comparison steals the only metric that matters: can your kid explain their project?

> To your kid: "Yours has your handwriting. That is the point."

> To the teacher: "How much parent help do you want on this one?"

One email saves a week of guessing.

## Pair with after-school timing

Big projects land after the [after-school collapse](/guides/the-after-school-collapse-is-not-a-bad-kid). Snack first, poster second. Starting hungry at 7 p.m. is how dads become art directors.

## Scripts

> To your kid: "You paste. I print. You tell the story."

> To yourself when perfection calls: "Their grade, their glue."

> To the room when it is ugly: "Ugly and done beats pretty and mine."

## The point

Whose science project is it anyway? If you would not sign your name, back up one step. Supply the board, guard the timer, and let the handwriting show. Broke does not mean broken. It means you saved Tuesday for sleep, not foam core.
